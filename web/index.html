Absys: programs written without specifying unnecessary order

An Absys text is a conjunction of assertions, not a sequence of instructions. As each one is accepted, whatever can be done is done; an equation between two unknowns is suspended until one of them is known; an or splits the computation into derivations that are all elaborated, fairly, until each fails or hibernates. Step through a text, or edit it: absys, compiled to WebAssembly, runs in this page.

What is reconstructed, and from what

The machine follows the account of its designers: E. W. Elcock's retrospective (J. Logic Programming, 1990), Foster and Elcock's introduction to Absys 1 (Machine Intelligence 4, 1969, as far as extracts of it survive) and Foster's "Assertions" (Machine Intelligence 3, 1968). Every rule, the page it rests on, and every place the sources are silent and a decision was made, is in docs/decisions.md.

Every computation the sources give a result for comes out as they say: the append refutation and its binding environment (X₁/1, L₁/[], N₁/[2], U/[2]); the reverse example that hibernates with U = X₃ and X₃ = V suspended, and wakes when rev([5], [V]) is added; Clark's students; setdiff with set; and, in the 1969 notation, x=y y=2, the alternatives that a=b+1 cuts down, item and the three triples.

The options under the text are not historical: they are there to study the rules beside them (see docs/research.md). With literals taken first in, first out, Elcock's own setdiff gives the wrong answer: not is not safe, and is then decided too soon.
